# Ludwig Koch and his method

Ask anywhere how to learn Morse code and one name comes back: the **Koch method**. Nearly every serious trainer of the last thirty years is built on it. Which makes it worth asking a question that is asked surprisingly rarely: what did Koch actually prove — and what do we merely believe he proved?

We went through the sources carefully before building our own trainer. Here is the honest picture.

## What Koch proposed

Ludwig Koch was a German psychologist who, in 1936, published a dissertation on the working psychology of Morse reception. His approach inverted the folk wisdom of his day. Instead of learning the whole alphabet slowly and speeding up — the path that reliably produces a painful plateau around ten words per minute — Koch had learners start with just **two characters at full target speed**. Only when a learner copied the current set with about **90 percent accuracy** was the next character added.

The two core insights have aged remarkably well. First: character speed and overall speed are different things, and the character's *sound shape* should be learned once, correctly, at its final tempo. Second: growth should be gated by demonstrated mastery, not by a calendar.

## What the record actually supports

Here the picture gets thinner than most retellings admit. Koch's dissertation is not available online, and nearly everything commonly cited about it — including the famous claim that his students reached about 12 WPM in under 14 hours of training — comes through **secondhand accounts**, most influentially a 1997 article by Dave Finley. Basic facts a modern reader would want, like the number of test subjects or a control group, are simply not on the public record. There are also hints the subjects were selected for aptitude.

Just as striking: we could find **no independent replication** of Koch's results in the decades since — not even from the method's advocates. The most rigorous Morse training research on record comes from elsewhere entirely: the psychologist Fred Keller's work for the US military in the 1940s. His successful "code-voice" method centered on something else — **immediate feedback** after every single character — and used neither Koch's incremental character introduction nor a 90 percent threshold. Keller later argued that the dreaded learning plateau is largely an artifact of poor training design, not a law of nature.

And one detail will surprise anyone who has used a Koch trainer: the canonical **character order starting K, M, R…** is, as far as the record shows, probably not Koch's at all. It appears to be a later addition from the trainer software era. Treating it as sacred has no evidential basis.

## So is the method good or not?

Both. The Koch method is best understood as a **sound didactic bet, not a proven system**. Its core — full-speed characters, mastery-gated growth — aligns well with what modern learning science has since established about skill acquisition. Its specifics — the exact 90 percent gate, the fixed character order, the pair-wise introduction — are conventions, and some of them look actively suboptimal today: a learner held at 95+ percent accuracy is being *parked*, not challenged, and research on optimal difficulty suggests the sweet spot for progress lies a little lower than the tradition assumes.
